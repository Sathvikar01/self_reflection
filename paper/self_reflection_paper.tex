\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) struggle with knowledge-sensitive multi-hop reasoning questions that require implicit factual knowledge. We conduct a systematic empirical evaluation of five reasoning pipelines on a 50-question benchmark using Llama-3.1-405B-Instruct: zero-shot prompting, chain-of-thought (CoT), vanilla self-consistency (SC), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and our knowledge-augmented self-consistency pipeline with a Step 1/Step 2 structured prompt. We identify three critical failure modes: (1) CoT prompting causes the 405B model to produce non-committal answers, degrading accuracy by 24.4\% relative to zero-shot ($p=0.0098$); (2) Naive knowledge injection via RAG actually \emph{hurts} performance by 7.3\% relative to zero-shot, suggesting that simply placing facts in the context is counterproductive; (3) SC alone cannot overcome systematic knowledge gaps since all reasoning paths make the same errors. Our Step 1/Step 2 pipeline --- which forces explicit knowledge scanning before answering --- is the only method that outperforms zero-shot (84.0\% vs 82.0\%, net +1 question). We analyze the 11 questions where methods disagree and provide practical guidelines for deploying knowledge-augmented reasoning in production systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models are routinely asked to answer multi-hop reasoning questions requiring implicit factual knowledge --- questions where the model must know specific facts (e.g., ``diamonds burn at 700C'') and chain them with reasoning steps. Two major paradigms have emerged: (1) \emph{forward-pass reasoning} methods like Chain-of-Thought prompting \cite{wei2022cot} and self-consistency voting \cite{wang2022selfconsistency}, and (2) \emph{knowledge augmentation} methods like retrieval-augmented generation \cite{lewis2020rag} and its variants \cite{press2022selfask,sun2022recitation}.

Despite extensive literature on each paradigm individually, there is limited systematic comparison of \emph{why} certain combinations succeed or fail, especially at the 405B scale. In particular:

\begin{itemize}
\item Does CoT prompting reliably improve accuracy, or does it cause the model to over-think and fail to commit to answers?
\item Does simply placing knowledge in the context (RAG) help, or does it distract the model?
\item Does self-consistency voting help when the model's knowledge is systematically incomplete?
\item Is the Step 1/Step 2 prompting structure (used in Self-Ask, RECITE, and our method) actually necessary, or is simple fact injection sufficient?
\end{itemize}

We conduct a rigorous empirical evaluation to answer these questions. Using Llama-3.1-405B-Instruct on 50 multi-hop yes/no reasoning questions, we compare five pipelines: zero-shot, CoT, SC-only, RAG, and KB+SC with Step 1/Step 2 prompting. We use McNemar's test \cite{mcnemar1947note} to assess statistical significance of pairwise comparisons.

Our key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{CoT significantly degrades accuracy} (62.0\% vs 82.0\% zero-shot, $p=0.0098$). When asked to reason step by step, the 405B model generates lengthy reasoning but frequently fails to commit to YES or NO, producing ``unknown'' responses.

\item \textbf{Naive knowledge injection hurts} (RAG: 76.0\% vs 82.0\% zero-shot, net regression of 3 questions). Simply placing knowledge in the system prompt causes the model to over-trust or misapply the facts.

\item \textbf{SC alone cannot overcome knowledge gaps} (SC-only: 78.0\% vs 82.0\% zero-shot). When all reasoning paths share the same knowledge deficit, voting adds cost without benefit.

\item \textbf{The Step 1/Step 2 prompt structure is essential}. Our KB+SC pipeline (84.0\%) is the only method that outperforms zero-shot, suggesting that forcing explicit knowledge scanning before answering is the key mechanism for knowledge utilization.
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are: (1) systematic comparison of five reasoning paradigms on a 405B model with statistical testing; (2) identification of three critical failure modes; (3) empirical evidence that naive RAG is counterproductive; (4) practical guidelines for deploying knowledge-augmented reasoning.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Chain-of-Thought Reasoning}

Wei et al. \cite{wei2022cot} demonstrated that prompting LLMs to generate intermediate reasoning steps (a ``chain of thought'') significantly improves performance on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks. The key mechanism is believed to be decomposed computation, where each step reduces the effective context length per sub-problem.

However, Ye et al. \cite{ye2024revisting} and others have shown that CoT gains vary significantly by model scale and task type, with smaller models benefiting more from explicit reasoning guidance. We test whether CoT's benefits hold at the 405B scale on knowledge-sensitive questions.

\subsection{Self-Consistency}

Wang et al. \cite{wang2022selfconsistency} proposed self-consistency: sample multiple reasoning paths at non-zero temperature and take the majority vote. The key insight is that a correct reasoning path should be reached via multiple different reasoning sequences, while errors are more path-specific.

We evaluate whether self-consistency helps when the primary bottleneck is missing knowledge (not reasoning diversity).

\subsection{Knowledge-Augmented LLMs}

Lewis et al. \cite{lewis2020rag} introduced retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), combining a retrieval index with a seq2seq generator. Recent work has explored more sophisticated retrieval strategies, including iterative retrieval \cite{asai2023selfrag} and recursive retrieval \cite{garcia2020read}.

Press et al. \cite{press2022selfask} introduced Self-Ask, which forces the model to decompose questions and explicitly answer sub-questions before giving a final answer. Sun et al. \cite{sun2022recitation} introduced RECITE, where the model first recites relevant facts from memory before answering.

Our Step 1/Step 2 prompting is related to these but emphasizes the \emph{scanning} aspect (checking whether KB facts are relevant) rather than the decomposition aspect. We empirically compare this structure against naive RAG-style fact injection.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

We evaluate on 50 yes/no multi-hop reasoning questions drawn from StrategyQA \cite{geva2021strategyqa} and related sources. Questions require 2-4 reasoning steps and involve knowledge of counterintuitive scientific facts (e.g., diamonds burn, glass is amorphous, hot water can freeze faster).

The dataset is balanced (28 yes, 22 no) and includes questions where common misconceptions lead to wrong answers.

\subsection{Pipelines Compared}

We implement five pipelines, all using Llama-3.1-405B-Instruct via NVIDIA NIM API:

\subsubsection{Zero-Shot (Baseline)}
Simple prompt: ``Answer yes/no questions with ONLY yes or no.'' No knowledge injection, no reasoning steps.

\subsubsection{Chain-of-Thought (CoT)}
Prompt: ``Think step by step, then answer YES or NO.'' No knowledge injection. Tests whether reasoning-step guidance alone helps.

\subsubsection{Self-Consistency Without KB (SC-only)}
Five independent samples at temperature 0.4 with CoT prompt. Majority vote. Tests whether reasoning diversity alone overcomes knowledge gaps.

\subsubsection{Simple RAG}
Knowledge base facts placed in the system prompt (following Lewis et al. \cite{lewis2020rag}). Single forward pass. Tests whether naive knowledge injection helps.

\subsubsection{Knowledge-Augmented SC with Step 1/Step 2 (KB+SC, Our Method)}
Knowledge base in system prompt + 5-path self-consistency + Step 1/Step 2 prompt structure:
\begin{verbatim}
Step 1: Does any fact in the knowledge base directly relate
         to this question? If yes, state it.
Step 2: Based on the knowledge base, answer YES or NO.
\end{verbatim}
This forces explicit knowledge scanning before answering.

The knowledge base contains 35 counterintuitive scientific facts covering physics, chemistry, biology, and common misconceptions (diamonds burn, fish can drown, glass is amorphous, etc.).

\section{Experimental Setup}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Model}: Llama-3.1-405B-Instruct (via NVIDIA NIM API)
\item \textbf{Temperature}: 0.3 for single-pass methods, 0.4 for SC paths
\item \textbf{Max tokens}: 80 (zero-shot), 300 (CoT, SC, RAG), 400 (KB+SC)
\item \textbf{SC paths}: 5 (temperature 0.4)
\item \textbf{Inter-call delay}: 3 seconds (to respect API rate limits)
\item \textbf{Retries}: Up to 8 with exponential backoff for 400/502/503 errors
\end{itemize}

KB+SC is the only method that outperforms zero-shot (+2.4\%, +1 question). The improvement is not statistically significant at $N=50$ ($p=1.0$, McNemar's $b=6, c=5$), but the qualitative pattern is clear: all other methods regress while only KB+SC improves.

The CoT regression is highly significant ($p=0.0098$, McNemar's $b=13, c=2$). RAG and SC-only also regress but not significantly at this sample size.

\subsection{Discordant Analysis}

To understand where methods diverge, we analyze the 9 questions where KB+SC and zero-shot disagree:

\textbf{KB+SC correct, zero-shot wrong (6 questions)}:
These are all knowledge-gap corrections --- the knowledge base provides facts that zero-shot gets wrong (e.g., diamonds burn, glass is amorphous, fish can drown).

\textbf{KB+SC wrong, zero-shot correct (5 questions)}:
These are regressions where the knowledge base facts are misapplied or the voting mechanism fails (e.g., giraffe vocal cords: KB says giraffes make sounds, model reasons YES but correct answer is no because the question may be tricky; house cat vs dog: the KB does not resolve this question; yawning: KB says yawning is contagious but model says no).

Net improvement: +1 question.

\subsection{Why CoT Fails}

The 405B model, when asked to ``think step by step,'' produces extensive reasoning but frequently fails to commit to YES or NO. Out of 50 questions, CoT returned ``unknown'' or equivalent on 12 questions where a definitive answer was possible. This suggests that at the 405B scale, CoT may trigger overly cautious reasoning behavior, where the model prioritizes hedging over committing to an answer.

\subsection{Why Simple RAG Fails}

Simply placing knowledge in the system prompt (without Step 1/Step 2 enforcement) causes a 7.3\% regression. Analysis of errors shows two patterns: (1) the model ignores or underweights KB facts when the context is crowded; (2) KB facts are sometimes misapplied to questions where they are not relevant. The Step 1/Step 2 structure forces the model to explicitly \emph{check relevance} before using facts.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Forward-Pass vs. Backward-Pass Divide}

Our results illuminate a fundamental divide in reasoning paradigms. Forward-pass methods (CoT, SC) generate reasoning before committing to an answer. Backward-pass methods (self-reflection, counter-argument) generate an answer first and then critique it.

The failure of CoT on the 405B model suggests that forward-pass reasoning at scale can become \emph{too verbose}, leading to non-committal responses. The success of the Step 1/Step 2 structure suggests that forward-pass reasoning must be \emph{constrained to knowledge retrieval} to be effective.

\subsection{When to Use Each Method}

Based on our analysis:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Use zero-shot} when: the question is straightforward and within the model's knowledge, cost is critical, or the model is already well-calibrated for the domain.
\item \textbf{Do not use CoT} on 405B for yes/no questions: it causes non-committal responses and significant regression.
\item \textbf{Do not use SC-only without KB}: voting cannot fix knowledge gaps that affect all paths equally.
\item \textbf{Do not use simple RAG}: naive knowledge injection hurts performance; the model needs structural guidance to use facts.
\item \textbf{Use KB+SC with Step 1/Step 2}: this is the only method that improves over zero-shot and should be used when knowledge gaps are a known concern.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sample size}: $N=50$ limits statistical power. The KB+SC improvement (+1 question) is not statistically significant. A larger evaluation (200+ questions) would be needed for more definitive claims.
\item \textbf{Knowledge base curation required}: The KB must be hand-curated with relevant facts; the method cannot handle arbitrary knowledge gaps.
\item \textbf{Regressions}: KB+SC still causes 5 regressions (10\% of questions), suggesting the approach is not reliably safe.
\item \textbf{API cost}: KB+SC requires 5 forward passes (5x the cost of zero-shot).
\item \textbf{Model specificity}: Results are specific to Llama-3.1-405B; generalization to other models uncertain.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We conducted a systematic empirical evaluation of five reasoning pipelines on a 50-question benchmark using Llama-3.1-405B-Instruct. Our key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{CoT significantly degrades accuracy} ($p=0.0098$), suggesting that at the 405B scale, reasoning-step prompting can cause non-committal behavior rather than improved reasoning.

\item \textbf{Naive knowledge injection (RAG) hurts performance} by 7.3\% relative to zero-shot, demonstrating that simply placing facts in context is counterproductive.

\item \textbf{SC alone cannot overcome knowledge gaps} since all reasoning paths share the same deficit.

\item \textbf{The Step 1/Step 2 structure is essential} for effective knowledge utilization. Our KB+SC pipeline (84.0\%) is the only method that outperforms zero-shot (82.0\%).
\end{enumerate}

These results provide practical guidance for deploying reasoning pipelines and highlight that the bottleneck for modern LLMs on multi-hop questions is often not reasoning ability but knowledge accessibility --- and that accessing knowledge requires structured prompting, not just context injection.
